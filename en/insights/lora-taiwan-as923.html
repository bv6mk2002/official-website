LoRa band planning: AS923 in Taiwan and how to choose SF and BW

Taiwan uses 920–925 MHz (AS923) for LoRa. This article explains the trade-offs between band, spreading factor (SF), bandwidth (BW), range and battery life — and what changes in other regions.

  2. 2. Spreading factor: range versus speed
  3. 3. Bandwidth and packet length

LoRa can send data several kilometers on very little power, which makes it a favorite for wide-area sensing. But with the same SX12xx chip, different settings can change range, data rate and battery life several times over. Here are the questions our customers ask most often.

1. Which band should you use?

LoRa is a license-free, low-power radio, and each region opens different bands:

RegionCommon channel planBand
TaiwanAS923920–925 MHz
USA, CanadaUS915902–928 MHz
EuropeEU868863–870 MHz
Mainland ChinaCN470470–510 MHz

Products sold in Taiwan must use 920–925 MHz and comply with the NCC low-power radio regulation (LP0002); other regions have their own rules, such as duty-cycle limits in Europe. If you plan to sell in several markets, decide on the bands early in hardware design, because antennas and matching circuits are designed for a specific frequency — different bands often need different component values or even a different antenna.

2. Spreading factor: range versus speed

LoRa spreading factors usually range from SF7 to SF12. Each step up roughly doubles the time per symbol:

  • Higher SF: better receiver sensitivity and longer range, but longer time on air and higher power consumption.
  • Lower SF: faster, lower power and more network capacity, but shorter range.

In practice we run a coverage test on site first and pick the SF that is just good enough, instead of defaulting to SF12. Sending the same data at SF12 can take tens of times longer on air than at SF7, which hurts both battery life and channel congestion.

3. Bandwidth and packet length

125 kHz is the most common bandwidth. A narrower bandwidth improves sensitivity but is slower; a wider one is the opposite. Also, keep packets short: binary encoding, sending only changes and batching several readings all reduce time on air considerably.

Tip: First decide how often you report and how many bytes each report contains, then work back to SF, BW and battery capacity. Data planning often affects battery life more than chip selection.

4. Antennas and mounting matter most for range

With identical settings, a poorly matched antenna or a node mounted inside a metal box can lose most of its range. A few general rules:

  • Mount gateway antennas as high as possible, away from metal obstructions.
  • Measure and tune node antennas inside the real enclosure, not just on a bare board.
  • Batteries, metal screws and LCDs can all detune the antenna.

For antenna design, see our article Choosing a PCB antenna: IFA, meander, patch and matching.

5. Point-to-point or LoRaWAN?

For a small number of nodes and a simple setup, a private point-to-point (P2P) protocol is quick and inexpensive to develop. When you have many nodes, need roaming between several gateways or want to join a public network, the standard LoRaWAN protocol is easier to scale and maintain. We recommend the right architecture based on project size and long-term operation.
